# FR-018 — POWER & TRUST: evidence and product-design memo

**Status:** research recommendation for editorial and moderated-prototype review; not approved pack copy<br>
**Evidence current through:** 17 August 2026<br>
**Product context assumed:** CLOSER is a shared-device conversation game for exactly two physically co-located adults. Spoken answers are not recorded, stored, or analyzed. Passing and ending remain available without explanation.

## Executive decision

**Conditional GO for a conversation-only prototype and moderated pilot. NO-GO for public release until the gates in this memo pass. NO-GO within FR-018 for physical tasks, technique teaching, scene facilitation, compatibility scoring, or persistent sexual-interest profiles.**

The defensible value proposition is modest and useful: give two adults neutral language for discussing *chosen* erotic power, roles, limits, stopping, and care without implying that either person wants to enact anything. Research supports the broader proposition that sexual communication is associated with sexual and relationship satisfaction, while BDSM-specific research describes negotiation, consent, role flexibility, and care as important participant practices. It does **not** show that this pack will improve trust, prevent harm, reveal compatibility, or make a risky practice safe.

The pack should therefore:

- treat fantasy, curiosity, discussion, role-play, permission, and consent to a specific action as separate states;
- explore roles without assigning them, testing them, or making gendered assumptions;
- make the intensity curve about disclosure and complexity, never about more extreme practices;
- require private, independent entry confirmation and a renewed opt-in before Act II;
- use a persistent, equal-weight **PASS** and **END** control, without score, countdown, persuasion, or explanation;
- contain no physical instruction and no question that invites an improvised scene;
- disable PLAYFUL mode and omit a private secret prompt;
- end with a neutral, optional reflection rather than a promise, plan, or “next step”; and
- be reviewed by kink-aware sexual-health, trauma, accessibility, safeguarding, bilingual editorial, legal/privacy, and product-safety reviewers before release.

The strongest working public name is **POWER, BY CHOICE**, with the descriptor **“A conversation about chosen roles, boundaries, and care / Ein Gespräch über freiwillig gewählte Rollen, Grenzen und Fürsorge.”** `POWER & TRUST` remains understandable as an internal feature-request label, but “trust” can sound like something a partner must prove and the phrase is broad and weakly distinctive. Naming requires separate trademark, store, domain, and localization clearance.

## 1. Scope, method, and evidence rules

### 1.1 Research question

Can CLOSER responsibly offer two adults a structured conversation about consensual erotic power and trust; if so, what content boundary, sequence, wording, interaction rules, exclusions, and validation gates are warranted?

This memo covers motivations; dominant/submissive/switch and top/bottom language; fantasy versus action; negotiation; explicit and implicit consent; stopping and change of mind; safewords, traffic-light language, check-ins, and aftercare; trust, vulnerability, intimacy, and sexual communication; stigma and pathologization; external power and dependency; intoxication and impaired consent; SSC, RACK, PRICK, and 4Cs; inclusion; and specialist-risk exclusions.

### 1.2 Method

This is a **targeted, source-driven review**, not a registered systematic review or meta-analysis. Searches prioritized peer-reviewed systematic/scoping reviews, meta-analyses, representative surveys, primary human research, PubMed records, DOI/publisher landing pages, and official diagnostic-classification sources. Community-origin sources were used only where the subject itself is community vocabulary or practice. Claims were checked against abstracts or full text where available and against stable DOI, PubMed, PMC, publisher, or WHO pages.

Direct evidence for a bilingual, shared-phone, two-person conversation game about erotic power was not located. Every UI, route, timing, and release recommendation is consequently an **evidence-informed product inference**, not a demonstrated intervention effect.

### 1.3 Evidence labels

| Label | Meaning | What it can support |
|---|---|---|
| **A — synthesis / probability sample** | Systematic or scoping review, meta-analysis, or large probability-based population study | Broad patterns, with the source's own heterogeneity and bias limits |
| **B — observational empirical** | Cross-sectional, longitudinal, experience-sampling, or field study, usually convenience-sampled | Associations and descriptions; not causal effects |
| **C — small qualitative / conceptual** | Interview, ethnographic, small niche study, or scholarly conceptual analysis | Vocabulary, mechanisms, lived experience, hypotheses; not prevalence |
| **D — community practice/history** | Community-developed norm, acronym, or convention without outcome validation | Editorial literacy only; never a safety certification |
| **P — product judgment** | CLOSER risk decision or design hypothesis | Must be tested; should not be presented as scientific fact |

“Community-developed” is not a synonym for ineffective. It means that widespread use or experiential value must not be misreported as proof of harm prevention. Likewise, absence of evidence is not evidence that a practice is harmless.

### 1.4 Transfer limits to keep visible

The literature is unusually heterogeneous in what counts as BDSM or kink. Much of it uses self-selected, online, community, or college samples; many samples are disproportionately White, educated, Western, English-speaking, cisgender, and/or coupled. Some studies intentionally recruit queer participants, yet that does not solve racial, disability, class, cultural, age, or non-monogamy gaps. Cross-sectional associations cannot establish that BDSM or communication caused an outcome. A finding from experienced community participants cannot be assumed to transfer to curious novices, coercive relationships, a shared-device setting, or all German- and English-speaking users.

## 2. Evidence and claim map

### 2.1 Mechanism hypothesis: why a conversation pack could be useful

| Proposed mechanism | Evidence anchor | What the pack actually does | Outcome it must **not** promise |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shared vocabulary may reduce ambiguity** | Consent has distinguishable internal and external dimensions, and role language varies by person/context (E4, E9). | Offers definitions as optional language, then asks users what fits or does not. | It does not reveal a “true” role or ensure identical meanings. |
| **State separation may interrupt category errors** | Fantasy prevalence exceeds enactment, and consent varies across contexts/events (E2, E10). | Repeats fantasy ≠ discussion permission ≠ action consent; keeps each answer local to the prompt. | It does not establish future willingness or legal consent. |
| **Voluntary disclosure may support understanding** | Communication meta-analyses and narrow dyadic self-disclosure research show associations/pathways through partner understanding (E7, E8, E23). | Gives a bounded turn and neutral listener responses while preserving general answers and PASS. | It does not guarantee intimacy, satisfaction, reciprocity, or benefit from disclosure. |
| **Making stopping discussable may clarify expectations** | Consent is ongoing/contextual; BDSM scholarship describes negotiation and violations (E10, E11). | Asks how maybe, change of mind, ordinary no/stop, and distress should be received. | It does not prevent violations or prove that either participant will behave accordingly. |
| **Separating chosen roles from outside power can surface voluntariness** | Authority-transfer scholarship identifies unequal exit costs as a consent concern (E12). | Names material leverage at entry and keeps everyday rights/equality outside any role. | It does not screen, diagnose, or resolve coercion or abuse. |
| **Privacy and non-pathologizing language can reduce product-created stigma** | Healthcare nondisclosure and minority-stress studies document stigma and concealment (E17–E20). | Stores no answer content/identity and permits hypothetical or disinterested responses. | It does not eliminate social stigma or confer a diagnosis of health. |

Trust is best treated here as a **possible consequence of consistently respectful behavior**, not a prerequisite to prove, a score, or a resource one partner owes. Vulnerability is an optional exposure that increases the product's duty to protect PASS, privacy, and non-retaliation; it is not an intensity metric. The app's immediate success target is comprehension and low-pressure communication, not intimacy itself.

### Bottom line from the map

The empirical base is strongest for **communication as an associated relationship process**, **within-person/contextual variability in consent**, **non-rarity and heterogeneity of interests**, and **documented stigma**. It is weaker for BDSM-specific causal mechanisms, safeword effectiveness, aftercare outcomes, acronym effectiveness, and app-mediated prevention. Evidence about self-disclosure supports possible pathways through partner understanding, not a “more is better” rule. The safest transfer is therefore to facilitate optional language and reflection while keeping forced/reciprocal disclosure, enactment, diagnosis, safety certification, and compatibility judgment outside scope.

## 3. Terminology and editorial usage

Use plain language first, then optional community terms. Never correct a participant's self-description unless needed to explain a safety distinction.

| Term | Working meaning for CLOSER | Editorial caution |
|---|---|---|
| **Kink** | Broad, culturally variable umbrella for non-normative erotic interests, practices, or identities. | Some people embrace it; others do not. Never assign it from an answer. |
| **BDSM** | An overlapping umbrella often expanded as bondage/discipline, dominance/submission, and sadism/masochism. | Boundaries and expansions vary. FR-018 concerns negotiated power, not the whole umbrella. |
| **Power exchange / Machtaustausch** | A voluntary, bounded arrangement in which people negotiate authority or control for a context. | “Exchange” must not imply irreversible transfer, ownership, or loss of ordinary rights. |
| **Dominant / submissive (D/s)** | Role or identity language concerning authority/control within a chosen dynamic. | Not gendered; not a personality diagnosis; capitalization preferences vary. |
| **Top / bottom** | Often describes who gives/directs versus receives/follows an activity or sensation. | Not necessarily dominant/submissive, relationship-wide, or fixed. |
| **Switch / fluid** | Someone whose role may vary, or who identifies with more than one role. | Variation may depend on partner, activity, setting, time, or none of these. |
| **Scene** | Community term for a bounded negotiated interaction, and sometimes for the wider community. | Ambiguous; avoid in core UI unless defined. FR-018 does not facilitate a scene. |
| **Fantasy** | An imagined scenario or theme. | Enjoying or sharing it does not imply desire to enact it. |
| **Discussion** | Permission to talk about an idea now. | It is not permission to plan, touch, role-play, or act. |
| **Role-play** | Consensual enactment within agreed boundaries. | Consent to one role-play is not blanket consent; FR-018 prompts no enactment. |
| **Interest / curiosity** | Something a person may like, wonder about, or wish to understand. | Not a commitment, identity, or yes. |
| **Permission** | Authorization for a defined conversational or other step. | Narrower than general willingness; it must not be silently expanded. |
| **Consent** | A voluntary, informed, specific, ongoing, and revocable agreement by a person with capacity. | A UI cannot certify capacity, voluntariness, legality, or future consent. An answer in this pack is never consent to an action. |
| **Hard limit / soft limit** | Community shorthand for something out of bounds versus conditional/uncertain. | Users may prefer “no,” “maybe,” or different language. A “soft” limit is not a challenge to overcome. |
| **Safeword / stop signal** | Pre-agreed word or signal used to slow, pause, or stop. | Ordinary “no,” “stop,” withdrawal, distress, silence, freezing, or inability to respond also require stopping/checking. |
| **Traffic-light system** | Common shorthand such as green/amber/red for continue/check or adjust/stop. | Meanings vary and must be agreed; it is not universal or fail-safe. |
| **Check-in** | A direct question about comfort, willingness, understanding, or need to pause. | Do not make the less-powerful role carry all monitoring responsibility. |
| **Aftercare** | Individually chosen post-intensity care or transition, possibly closeness, space, practical care, quiet, or later contact. | Not mandatory in one form, not only for a submissive/bottom, and not a medical treatment. |
| **Coercion** | Pressure or constraint that makes refusal costly or unsafe, including threats, retaliation, manipulation, or material leverage. | It can exist within a loving relationship and without physical force. The app cannot adjudicate abuse. |
| **Capacity / impairment** | Ability to understand, choose, communicate, and revise a decision in context. | Law varies by jurisdiction. Do not use the app while intoxicated or otherwise impaired; do not claim the app assessed capacity. |
| **SSC** | *Safe, Sane and Consensual*, community language commonly traced to 1980s gay male S/M organizing. | “Safe” and “sane” are subjective and can stigmatize; no empirical certification. |
| **RACK** | *Risk-Aware Consensual Kink*, a later community formulation emphasizing acknowledged risk. | Awareness is not expertise, voluntariness, or adequate mitigation. |
| **PRICK** | Commonly expanded as *Personal Responsibility, Informed, Consensual Kink*. | Canonical origin was not verified; “personal responsibility” can become victim-blaming after a violation. |
| **4Cs** | *Caring, Communication, Consent and Caution*, proposed as another framework. | Helpful prompts, not validated outcome criteria. |

### Framework comparison

| Framework | Useful editorial contribution | Material limitation | CLOSER treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| SSC | Centers consent and an aspiration to avoid unreasonable harm. | “Safe” can conceal residual risk; “sane” is vague and pathologizing. | May appear only in optional literacy copy, with caveat. |
| RACK | Makes residual risk and informed discussion more visible. | People can misunderstand risk; knowledge and bargaining power may be unequal. | Prefer its questions—“What is known? What is uncertain?”—not the badge. |
| PRICK | Emphasizes information and agency. | Can individualize systemic/coercive risk and be misused to blame harmed people. Origin and standardization are weak. | Do not use as a release or consent standard. |
| 4Cs | Adds care and communication and avoids “safe/sane.” | Still community/scholarly proposal without intervention validation. | Most compatible as editorial values, but do not brand the pack as “4Cs compliant.” |

**Product rule:** no acronym unlocks content, proves readiness, or replaces an exact conversation about person, action, setting, risk, signals, stopping, and care.

**Origin note (Level D):** community histories commonly attribute SSC wording to David Stein's work with Gay Male S/M Activists in 1983 and RACK to Gary Switch in a TES-Friends discussion in 1999. The original community records are not stable peer-reviewed evidence, so those attributions should be presented as community history, not settled scientific provenance. PRICK appears in later community usage, but this review did not locate a canonical first use. The 4Cs proposal is traceable to the 2014 Williams et al. article. None of the four has controlled outcome validation.

## 4. Risk, exclusion, and response matrix

| Risk or context | Severity / likelihood in this format | V1 decision | Required copy or behavior | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| One person cannot safely refuse, pass, or end | Critical / context-dependent | **Do not start; immediate End** | “If saying no or ending could bring punishment, retaliation, or danger, do not use this pack.” Offer neutral support/resources, not mediation. | Formal yes is insufficient when exit costs are unsafe. |
| Intoxication, drug effects, cognitive impairment, sleep deprivation, or acute dissociation | Critical / context-dependent | **Exclude for now** | Both privately confirm they are not intoxicated or otherwise impaired. | CLOSER cannot assess capacity; consent cues become less reliable. |
| Material/institutional leverage | Critical / lower-frequency but high-impact | **Exclude this pairing when refusal could threaten essentials** | Name work authority, housing, care, immigration status, essential finances, clinical/educational authority as examples. | External dependency can undermine voluntariness; this is a conservative product screen, not a validated diagnostic. |
| Active conflict, fear, recent violation, acute distress, or use as “relationship repair” | High | **Defer / End** | “This is not mediation or crisis support. Come back only if both feel safe and unpressured.” | A provocative shared-device exchange can amplify pressure and disclosure risk. |
| Fantasy or discussion treated as permission | Critical / foreseeable | **Mitigate repeatedly** | Persistent banner or short footer: “An answer is not consent to an action.” Include separation response card. | Central category error for the pack. |
| Role assignment, essentialism, or gender stereotyping | High / foreseeable | **Prohibit** | Never infer “you are the dominant/submissive,” pair role with gender, or require choosing a label. | Roles are heterogeneous and can be contextual/fluid. |
| “Trust test,” compatibility test, score, prediction, or lie detection | High / foreseeable | **Prohibit** | No scores, reveals, matches, “prove it,” partner prediction, or result profile. | Converts disclosure into performance and increases retaliation/shame risk. |
| Persuasion after no/maybe/pass | Critical / foreseeable | **Prohibit** | Equal PASS/END; no follow-up card after pass; no “why not?”; no streak loss or negative animation. | A maybe or limit is not an obstacle to overcome. |
| Safeword overconfidence | High | **Mitigate** | State special words are optional tools, ordinary stop/no always count, and distress/inability to respond requires stopping. | Safewords are not proven fail-safe and depend on ability to communicate. |
| Hazardous specialist practice | Critical | **No generated prompt or instruction** | Exclude breath/neck compression, restraint/immobilization/suspension, impact/marking, electrical/fire/heat, sharps/blood, chemicals/drugs, gags or sensory deprivation that impair stopping, medical play, and trauma reenactment. | Severity, hidden injury, skill, equipment, and emergency-response issues exceed a conversation app. |
| Improvised physical escalation | Critical / foreseeable | **Conversation-only V1** | No touch, position, command, voice-performance, dress, prop, dare, demonstration, rehearsal, or “try it now.” | A discussion can become de facto scene facilitation even without explicit sex acts. |
| Disclosure of trauma, abuse, illegal conduct, or third-party secrets | High | **Do not solicit** | Prompts permit general/hypothetical answers; never ask “when were you violated?” or require examples/names. | The app cannot provide trauma care, mandatory-reporting guidance, or privacy guarantees. |
| Outing, shared-device residue, screenshots, analytics, logs | High | **Data-minimize** | Store no answer content, labels, interests, gate choice, or sexual profile; transient phase state only; neutral app-switcher/notification copy. | Stigma and material consequences of disclosure are documented. |
| Protected-trait degradation or race-play generation | High | **Prohibit** | No prompts involving race, ethnicity, disability, religion, gender identity, sexual orientation, migration status, or other protected traits as degradation material. | Distinct discrimination/fetishization risks; not necessary to the learning goal. |
| Access barriers: vision, motor, hearing, literacy, language, neurodivergence, chronic pain | Medium/high | **Design and test** | Screen reader, scalable type, reduced motion, no timed input, plain-language glossary, text alternative to color, flexible pacing, optional read-aloud with privacy warning. | Direct evidence for this pack is absent; inclusion is an ethical and usability requirement. |
| Mismatch followed by shame or retaliation | High | **Neutralize** | Closing copy normalizes different interests and stopping; no verdict or advice to compromise. | The app must not adjudicate compatibility or repair a harmful reaction. |
| Under-18 access | Critical | **Adult-only** | Independent 18+ confirmation at entry and before Act II; no youth framing or age-play content. | Product and subject boundary; a checkbox is not age verification. |

### What an exclusion screen can and cannot do

It can set a product boundary and give a user language to leave. It cannot detect coercion, confirm sobriety, assess legal capacity, establish that a relationship is safe, or make an unsafe person safe. Copy must avoid “You are safe to continue.” The strongest truthful state is: **“Both people selected Continue. You can still pass or end at any time.”**

## 5. Recommended pack architecture

### 5.1 Product promise

> A structured conversation about voluntarily chosen erotic roles, boundaries, stopping, and care. It does not instruct activities, assess compatibility, or turn an answer into consent.

### 5.2 Entry and escalation flow

1. **Adult/context screen:** public shared explanation: adults only; conversation only; not for intoxication, impaired decision-making, fear, active conflict, crisis, or a context where refusal could endanger safety or essentials.
2. **Private opt-in A:** device handoff; `CONTINUE` and `NOT NOW` have equal visual weight. No reason field.
3. **Private opt-in B:** same copy and controls. Keep each choice transient; show only a neutral group outcome.
4. **Neutral outcome:** if either declines, “Not tonight. Nothing needs explaining.” Offer ordinary pack selection and End. Do not reveal who declined or invite another attempt immediately.
5. **Act I:** curiosity and language; least personal.
6. **Renewed private opt-in before Act II:** “The next act discusses agreements, limits, and stopping more directly. Continue?” Repeat independently. A decline produces the same neutral outcome.
7. **Acts II and III:** PASS and END remain persistent and equal. No further escalating gate is required because content never progresses to action; users may still end at every screen.
8. **Optional finale:** direct shared reflection, then End. No stored result.

Private gates reduce but cannot remove shared-room inference or pressure. Pilot observation must test whether the handoff itself feels unsafe, performative, or easy to monitor.

### 5.3 Three-act progression

| Act | Working title | Editorial aim | Allowed prompt types | Ceiling / what must not appear |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | **NEUGIER / CURIOSITY** | Establish personal meanings, optionality, fantasy/action separation, and flexible role language. | General, hypothetical, vocabulary, assumptions, preferred conversational tone. | No demand to identify a role, reveal experience, name a fantasy, or declare interest. |
| II | **ABSPRACHE / NEGOTIATION** | Discuss how a welcome conversation, maybe/no, boundaries, check-ins, stop signals, revisability, and outside power would be handled. | Process and hypothetical agreements; exact distinction between talk and action. | No live contract, action plan, technique, props, touch, commands, demonstrations, or “what would you let me do?” |
| III | **FÜRSORGE / CARE** | Explore receiving a stop, after-conversation needs, privacy, mismatch, equality outside roles, and repair principles. | Reflective and relational, with hypothetical option. | No promise to enact, disclosure of violation/trauma, compatibility verdict, or pressure to reconcile. |

The curve moves from **semantic safety → process clarity → relational vulnerability**. “Deeper” means more reflective, not more explicit, risky, or physically intense.

### 5.4 Routes and timing hypotheses

| Route | Count | Composition | Pilot timing hypothesis | Recommendation |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| Quick | 12 | 4 per act | 18–28 minutes | Useful low-burden route after Standard is stable; it still contains all core safety concepts. |
| Standard | 24 | 8 per act | 35–55 minutes | **Default and first route to pilot.** Best balance for comprehension without the fatigue of 36 questions. |
| Full | 36 | 12 per act | 55–85 minutes | For willing users after route-specific pilot; show expected time and remind users they may stop. |

These are product hypotheses based on roughly 1–3 minutes of spoken exchange per prompt plus handoffs and gates, not measured durations. Do not add a timer. Collect route completion time and fatigue in the pilot; shorten by removing prompts, not by rushing answers.

### 5.5 Question-design principles

- One cognitive job per question.
- Ask for meanings, preferences, and processes; not confessions, proof, or plans.
- Use “if anything,” “you may answer generally,” and “or none” where they protect genuine options.
- Keep **you** questions answerable without disclosing past partners, trauma, or practice history.
- Preserve answer symmetry: interest, uncertainty, disinterest, and pass are equally valid.
- Name both parties' responsibility for attention, stopping, and care; do not make a submissive/bottom responsible for preventing harm.
- Use community vocabulary only with a plain-language alternative.
- Distinguish role inside a chosen context from equality, rights, and ordinary decision-making outside it.
- Treat consent as action-, person-, context-, and time-specific and revisable.
- Phrase risk information plainly without sensationalizing or teaching hazardous methods.
- Localize concepts, not word order; DE and EN must each receive independent kink-aware editorial review.

### 5.6 Prohibited prompt and interaction patterns

Do not ship any variant of:

- “Which one of you is really dominant?”
- “Guess what your partner would let you do.”
- “Prove you trust them by…”
- “No thinking—answer now.”
- “Both reveal your hard limit at the same time.”
- “A good submissive/dominant should…”
- “What happened to make you like this?”
- “Tell your partner your darkest fantasy.”
- “Choose a punishment/reward/command.”
- “Try this position, restraint, impact, breath, pain, humiliation, or sensory task.”
- “If you use a safeword, the activity is safe.”
- “You matched; you are compatible.”
- countdowns, streaks, scores, escalating unlocks, seductive error states, celebratory disclosure rewards, prediction/reveal mechanics, or follow-up pressure after PASS.

### 5.7 Participation and escalation rules

- Both participants independently confirm 18+, present capacity, absence of intoxication/impairment, and ability to pass/end without retaliation.
- Visibility preference in settings is not session consent. Previous completion is not current consent.
- A “yes” to the pack means only “yes to seeing and discussing the current prompt.”
- A spoken answer is not stored, scored, interpreted, or used to personalize later prompts.
- A PASS skips immediately and silently. It disables response cards for that prompt.
- END is always present and never asks “Are you sure?” more than once; preferably it ends immediately with neutral copy.
- No prompt may require a reciprocal disclosure. One person may answer generally while the other answers personally.
- Do not retry a declined Act II gate in the same session.
- If a participant indicates fear, retaliation, incapacity, or a crossed boundary, the product ends the pack and offers neutral support options. It does not question the other person, decide what occurred, or mediate.

### 5.8 Private moment

Set `privateMoment: none` for content. The only asymmetric screens are the safety/readiness gates. A secret sexual question, instruction, or “surprise” task would create pressure, privacy residue, and an asymmetry that cannot be justified for this learning goal.

### 5.9 Finale

Use one optional direct reflection, not a catalog question and not a saved result:

> **DE:** Was würde ein zukünftiges Gespräch über freiwillig gewählte Macht für dich klarer und sicherer machen? Ihr müsst heute nichts entscheiden oder ausprobieren.<br>
> **EN:** What would make a future conversation about chosen power feel clearer and safer for you? You do not need to decide or try anything today.

Controls: `ANSWER / ANTWORTEN`, `PASS / PASSEN`, and `END / BEENDEN` with equal dignity. Do not generate a plan, contract, matching score, or reminder after the answer.

### 5.10 Response-card grammar

Allow at most two optional response cards after selected non-safety prompts. Never show them after PASS or a safety disclosure.

| Card | DE | EN | Constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| **KLÄREN / CLARIFY** | „Sag kurz, was du verstanden hast. Die sprechende Person darf es korrigieren.“ | “Briefly say what you heard. Let the speaker correct it.” | No interpretation or diagnosis. |
| **ANNEHMEN / RECEIVE** | „Ein Ja, Vielleicht, Nein oder Passen braucht keine Überredung.“ | “A yes, maybe, no, or pass needs no persuasion.” | Acknowledgment only; no follow-up demand. |
| **TRENNEN / SEPARATE** | „Benennt: Geht es um Fantasie, Neugier, ein Gespräch oder etwas, das vielleicht später besprochen werden könnte?“ | “Name whether this is fantasy, curiosity, discussion, or something that might be discussed later.” | Never add an “action” shortcut. |
| **NACHFRAGEN / CHECK** | „Stell eine neutrale Verständnisfrage. Verhandelt jetzt keine Handlung.“ | “Ask one neutral question for understanding. Do not negotiate an action now.” | Not for limits, passes, distress, or disclosures of harm. |

Do not use generic “Go deeper,” “Challenge,” “Convince,” “Reveal,” or `VALIDATE` cards. “Validate” can accidentally sound as though the app endorses an unsafe proposal; the safe target is receiving the person, not certifying the content.

### 5.11 PLAYFUL compatibility

**Disabled for V1 and incompatible with the current PLAYFUL mechanic.** Prediction, simultaneous reveal, speed, “both,” dares, performance, scoring, and teasing penalties all make consent and identity answers more coercive or easier to misread. A future light-tone presentation could use warmth or gentle humor in transitions, but it must remain a separate editorial skin with identical safety controls—not PLAYFUL mechanics.

### 5.12 Optional physical action: future architecture only

Physical action is **outside FR-018**. It should not enter as an “optional card” after editorial review. Any future product considering embodied prompts needs its own feature classification and evidence/safety work, including:

- per-action, private, bilateral and transient opt-in immediately before the action;
- exact action, body area, duration, intensity, foreseeable risks, accessibility alternative, and stop method;
- equal `YES`, `ADJUST`, `NEXT`, and `STOP` controls with no remembered preference;
- specialist review appropriate to the action, jurisdiction, disability access, trauma, and emergency response;
- no action that compromises speech, breathing, circulation, mobility, consciousness, or the ability to use END; and
- separate pilot and incident gates.

That architecture still would not make specialist-risk practices suitable. Breath/neck compression and the other critical exclusions in the matrix remain prohibited.

## 8. Moderated pilot and validation plan

### 8.1 Review before participant exposure

Obtain documented review from:

1. a kink-aware sexual-health clinician or researcher;
2. a consent/sexual-violence or trauma-informed safeguarding specialist;
3. an accessibility researcher covering screen-reader, cognitive, motor, visual, hearing, and language access;
4. independent native DE and EN editors familiar with non-pathologizing kink language;
5. privacy/security and product-safety reviewers; and
6. legal counsel for adult content, consumer protection, duty-to-warn/resource copy, data minimization, localization, and naming.

Community review should include experienced dominant, submissive, switch/fluid, non-role-identifying, queer, trans/nonbinary, racially varied, disabled/chronic-pain, novice/curious, and “not interested” perspectives. Community acceptability complements rather than substitutes for safeguarding and empirical review.

### 8.2 Stage A — cognitive and safety-critical usability pilot

**Suggested sample:** 12–16 dyads, purposively varied rather than claimed representative; DE and EN sessions; a mix of established couples/partners, novices, experienced community participants, different role relations, genders, orientations, relationship structures, ages, cultures, disabilities/access needs, and levels of interest. Exclude anyone who cannot freely decline participation; do not recruit manager/direct-report, clinician/client, teacher/student, caregiver-dependent, or other materially leveraged dyads.

**Protocol:**

- consent separately for research participation; make research withdrawal independent of partner and product END;
- run the Standard route first with a moderator observing interaction mechanics, not answer content;
- do **not** audio-record, transcribe, or code intimate spoken answers;
- collect only operational events needed for the test: finding PASS/END, gate choices, route time, requests for clarification, skips, exits, and minimal incident category/severity;
- interview each participant privately after the session so the other person cannot shape the report;
- use teach-back: “What did agreeing to the pack mean?” “Does an answer permit an action?” “What happens after maybe, pass, or stop?” “Could the app tell whether consent was valid?”;
- explicitly test screen-reader flow, text scaling, reduced motion, color-independent traffic language, one-handed/motor access, reading load, and app-switcher/notification privacy; and
- offer a voluntary private follow-up 24–72 hours later about delayed discomfort, pressure, conflict, or benefit, without asking for sexual content.

### 8.3 Stage B — route and bilingual field pilot

After Stage A issues are resolved and retested, pilot Standard with approximately 24–36 additional dyads, then separately pilot Quick and Full. These are pragmatic discovery sizes, not power calculations. Predefine revision thresholds; do not treat an average satisfaction score as proof of safety.

Track:

- critical-copy comprehension and teach-back;
- PASS and END discoverability and time-to-use;
- perceived pressure to answer, continue, disclose, agree, or later enact (0–10 plus private free text);
- whether “trust,” “power,” role words, or response cards felt like a test or diagnosis;
- clarity of fantasy/discussion/action separation;
- clarity that stop/no counts without a safeword;
- emotional load, fatigue, attention to the phone, and route duration;
- gate decline, pass, abandonment, and neutral-ending response;
- perceived inclusion, language naturalness, access barriers, and privacy concerns; and
- adverse events and patterns by language, experience, role, gender, sexuality, race/ethnicity, disability/access need, and relationship structure where sample size and privacy permit. Do not publish misleading subgroup percentages from tiny cells.

### 8.4 Adverse-event protocol

If either participant signals distress, fear, retaliation, inability to end, unwanted contact, or a consent concern:

1. stop the product session immediately;
2. offer separate space/interview if the person wants and it is safe;
3. do not mediate, confront the other participant, investigate facts, or ask for unnecessary details;
4. follow the research site's safeguarding and emergency protocol and offer jurisdiction-appropriate resources;
5. record only the minimum category, severity, product state, and copy/prompt involved; and
6. convene product-safety review before further exposure if the event may be product-attributable.

### 8.5 Go/no-go gates

**GO to broader beta only when all are true:**

- 100% of participants in the final retest round can locate PASS and END and correctly teach back that an answer is not action consent;
- 100% correctly understand that consent can change and that ordinary no/stop or distress requires stopping, with no special-word loophole;
- no serious product-attributable adverse event remains unresolved;
- no repeated interpretation of the pack, title, finale, or response cards as a trust test, compatibility test, live negotiation, or promise to enact;
- private gates can be completed accessibly and without the design revealing who declined;
- DE and EN receive independent approval for meaning equivalence and naturalness;
- no consistent pressure/access problem is concentrated in a reviewed subgroup;
- every prompt has a documented safety disposition and owner; and
- clinical/consent, trauma/safeguarding, accessibility, privacy/security, legal, and RaDi product owners sign off.

**Automatic redesign and retest:** one critical misunderstanding of answer-versus-consent, one case where END cannot be used privately/accessibly, one prompt-induced physical action, repeated pressure after maybe/pass, or any serious product-attributable incident. Do not average a critical failure away.

**NO-GO regardless of satisfaction scores:** physical tasks or technique advice; hazardous-practice content; PLAYFUL pressure mechanics; saved answers/interests; compatibility or role assignment; age-play; external-dependency pairing; or claims that the pack improves trust, prevents violations, or makes kink safe.

| Candidate | Strengths | Risks | Disposition |
|---|---|---|---|
| **POWER, BY CHOICE** | Puts voluntariness in the title; distinguishes chosen role from imposed power; reasonably distinctive in a preliminary exact-phrase web scan. | English comma construction is less immediate in German; could sound campaign-like without descriptor. | **Recommended for concept and clearance testing.** |

## 10. Release claims and prohibited claims

### Defensible, carefully bounded copy

- “A guided conversation about chosen roles, boundaries, stopping, and care.”
- “You can answer generally, pass, or end without explaining.”
- “An answer is never consent to an action.”
- “People use different words and can want different things—or not be interested.”
- “Consent is specific, ongoing, and can change.”

### Do not claim

- “Scientifically proven to build trust/intimacy.”
- “Makes BDSM safe” or “prevents consent violations.”
- “Reveals your true role,” “finds your match,” or “tests compatibility.”
- “A safeword guarantees safety.”
- “Aftercare is biologically necessary” or a fixed protocol.
- “BDSM practitioners are healthier,” “kink heals trauma,” or “trauma causes kink.”
- “SSC/RACK/PRICK/4Cs compliance means valid consent.”
- “Both users consented,” when the app has only received button selections.

## 11. Evidence gaps and research backlog

1. No direct outcome or safety validation exists for this shared-device pack.
2. No validated screening tool was found for deciding whether a dyad can safely discuss erotic power in a consumer app.
3. Safeword, traffic-light, and aftercare research is descriptive and does not establish prevention efficacy or universal best practice.
4. Community-framework origin records are incomplete and sometimes preserved in unstable archives; PRICK's canonical origin was not verified.
5. Disability, neurodivergence, race, culture, class, older-adult, non-monogamy, and non-Western evidence is too sparse for confident universal transfer.
6. German-language comprehension and the cultural fit of imported English role terms require original testing, not translation review alone.
7. Shared-device privacy and independent opt-in under co-location need direct observation and private post-session reporting.
8. Product effects beyond immediate comprehension—later pressure, conflict, unwanted enactment, beneficial communication, or stigma reduction—are unknown.

These gaps support a narrow prototype, conservative exclusions, and strong pilot gates; they do not support expanding scope.
